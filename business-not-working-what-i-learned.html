Trying to Build a Business That Wasn’t Working: What I Learned Along the Way

What believing in business ideas, struggling to gain traction, spending money trying to fix them, and questioning myself taught me about understanding a business before continuing to guess.

A Lesson I Learned the Expensive Way
“I wanted something I wish I had earlier: a way to stop guessing and understand what the business was actually telling me.”
Tracy Clinscales · Founder, Business & Brand Intelligence
I know what it feels like to believe in an idea, put real effort into it, spend money trying to make it work, and still sit there wondering why the sales are not coming.

I have tried to start businesses before.

I have had ideas I believed in. I have spent weeks and months working on them. I have built things, researched, created websites, tried to market them, and spent money that I honestly did not have to waste.

And then I waited for the part everyone talks about.

The customers. The sales. The moment when all of that work finally starts turning into a business.

Sometimes, that moment didn't come.

And when you have real responsibilities on the other side of every business decision, failure doesn't feel like an interesting entrepreneurial experiment.

Every dollar matters.

Every hour matters.

So when something I built wasn't gaining traction, I didn't just wonder: “What's wrong with the business?”

I wondered: “Maybe I'm not supposed to be doing this.”

But after everything I have learned, I don't believe that anymore.

I have come to understand that a business not working the way I hoped did not automatically tell me why it wasn't working.

That distinction changed the way I think about business.

Wanting a business and building one are two different things

I know what it feels like to want something of your own.

To have an idea and imagine what it could become.

To picture customers finding it. Sales coming in. The business growing. Maybe even reaching a point where something you created changes your financial life.

That possibility is exciting.

But then you actually start building.

You discover how many decisions sit between the idea and the business you imagined.

1
What should I call it?
2
Who exactly is it for?
3
What should I charge?
4
How do people find me?
5
Why are people looking but not buying?
6
Is the business idea wrong — or am I simply doing something wrong?

When you don't know the answer, it is very easy to start trying everything.

One of my biggest business lessons came from spending money without enough clarity

I know what it feels like to believe in an idea and still not understand why people aren't buying.

That uncertainty can make you start reaching for solutions.

Maybe I need a better website. Maybe I need to advertise. Maybe I need to post more. Maybe I need different branding. Maybe my price is wrong. Maybe I need another platform. Maybe I need new software. Maybe I need to completely change the business.

Or maybe the idea itself is bad.

The problem is that all of those things can cost time or money. And if you haven't identified the actual problem, you can spend a lot trying to solve the wrong one.

I know because I have done it.

That experience eventually became part of the reason I built Business & Brand Intelligence.

I wanted something I wish I had earlier:

A way to stop guessing and understand what the business was actually telling me.

Working hard doesn't automatically tell you what is wrong

This is the part of entrepreneurship that deserves more honesty.

You can love your idea and still have a business that doesn't work.

You can work incredibly hard and still struggle to make sales.

You can have a beautiful website and very few customers.

You can post every day and still not convert attention into revenue.

You can have an excellent product that not enough people know exists.

Or you can have plenty of people discovering you, but something in the offer, positioning, customer journey, or buying process is preventing them from moving forward.

That does not automatically mean your dream is bad.

But it also doesn't mean you should keep pouring money into it indefinitely simply because you believe in it.

At some point, belief needs information.

“My business isn't growing” is a symptom, not an explanation

One of the biggest things I have learned is that the same frustrating result can come from very different problems.

If customers cannot find you, you may have a visibility problem.

If customers find you but don't understand the offer, you may have a positioning problem.

If they understand the offer but don't buy, there may be a conversion problem.

If they express interest and disappear, there may be a follow-up or customer-journey problem.

If customers are buying but you are overwhelmed delivering the work, there may be an operational or capacity problem.

Those problems can all produce the same sentence: “My business isn't growing.”

But they do not require the same solution.

That's why the question should not always be: “What should I do next?”

Sometimes the better question is: “What is actually happening here?”

This is why I built Business & Brand Intelligence

My own experiences taught me something that now sits at the center of Business & Brand Intelligence.

When a business isn't working, the owner often wants someone to tell them what to do next.

Run ads. Post more. Change the website. Lower the price. Buy another tool. Try another strategy.

But before prescribing another solution, I believe there is a more important question:

What evidence do we have about where the business is actually breaking down?

Because “I'm not getting enough sales” is a symptom.

It doesn't automatically tell you the cause.

Maybe not enough people know the business exists.

Maybe people are finding it but don't understand why they should buy.

Maybe the offer isn't strong enough.

Maybe people want it but something is getting in the way of purchasing.

Maybe leads aren't being followed up.

Maybe customers buy but don't return.

Maybe the business is selling but operations are consuming so much of the owner's time that growth becomes difficult.

Different problems require different solutions.

And if your time and money matter—and for most of us they do— you cannot afford to spend endlessly trying every possible solution.

That lesson came directly from my own experience.

You do not need to build an empire for your business to matter

I also want to challenge one idea I see constantly in entrepreneurship.

Everything has to be huge.

Seven figures. Eight figures. Thousands of customers. Hundreds of thousands of followers. Massive scale.

But what if your business eventually generates enough money to pay one major household bill?

That matters.

What if it gives you another income stream?

That matters.

What if it eventually replaces your salary?

That matters.

What if it gives you flexibility you didn't have before?

That matters.

What if the first business fails but teaches you what you need to build the second?

That matters too.

You get to decide what successful entrepreneurship means for your life.

If you have an idea you still believe in

You do not need permission to explore it.

You don't have to quit your job tomorrow.

You don't need to empty your savings account.

You don't need a perfect website.

You don't need to know exactly what the business will look like five years from now.

Start by learning.

1
Who needs what you want to create — and why?
2
How are they solving that problem now?
3
Would they pay for your solution?
4
What would it cost you to provide it?
5
Can you test the idea on a smaller scale?
6
What happens when you put it in front of real people?

Learn before you spend heavily.
Test before you scale.
Measure before you assume.

And when something doesn't work, don't immediately translate that into: “I can't do this.”

Ask: “What is this teaching me?”

Sometimes the next step isn't doing more

I have tried things that didn't work.

I have spent money I wish I had spent differently.

I have questioned my ideas.

I have questioned myself.

But those experiences also taught me to stop asking only: “What else should I try?”

And start asking: “What do I need to understand?”

That question changed the way I think about business.

And eventually, it became part of the reason Business & Brand Intelligence exists.

Because sometimes the first thing your business needs isn't more money.

More marketing.

More content.

More software.

Or another idea.

Sometimes you need to understand the business you already have.

And sometimes, understanding what's happening is what gives you the confidence to keep building.

Tracy Clinscales
Founder, Business & Brand Intelligence
